> title: The Carbon War Hiding Inside Every Green Certification
There's a small metal plaque in thousands of building lobbies around the world. LEED. EDGE. IGBC. GRIHA.
People walk past it every day assuming it means roughly the same thing everywhere. It doesn't.
And the differences between these systems say more about how climate action actually works than most people realise.

# scene photos=world_map grade=night
> chapter: 1 · Four plaques, four philosophies
Let's start with what each one actually is, because the names get thrown around as if they were interchangeable.

# scene photos=us_skyline grade=day
> label: LEED · U.S. Green Building Council
> stat: 150+ | countries and territories with LEED projects | USGBC, 2015 @ hundred and fifty
LEED was created by the U.S. Green Building Council. It's the most internationally recognised system, with projects in more than a hundred and fifty countries and territories.
It's point-based and comprehensive, which is also its challenge. A scoring system born in the American market has to stretch to fit a monsoon climate in Mumbai or a desert site in the Gulf.
It works, but sometimes it works like a translated document. You can tell it wasn't first written in the local language.

That isn't a criticism of LEED so much as a fact of geography. Climate, construction practice, and energy prices change what "efficient" even means.

# scene photos=subcontractors:2 grade=day
> label: EDGE · IFC, World Bank Group
> item: ≥20% less energy @ energy
> item: ≥20% less water @ water
> item: ≥20% less embodied energy in materials @ materials
EDGE exists because of that gap. It was created by IFC, part of the World Bank Group, to make green certification fast and affordable in emerging markets.
It strips the question down to three numbers that matter most in cost-sensitive construction. Energy. Water. And the embodied energy in materials. To be certified, a building has to beat a local baseline by at least twenty percent on all three.

# scene photos=site_walk grade=day
> stat: 100M+ m² | of floor space certified by EDGE (reported 2025) | IFC / press reports @ hundred million
It's less famous outside the industry. But by 2025, more than a hundred million square metres of floor space had reportedly been certified, much of it in places the older systems rarely reached.

# scene photos=india_skyline grade=day
> label: IGBC · Indian Green Building Council (CII)
> label: GRIHA · developed by TERI @ GRIHA
Then there are the home-grown systems. IGBC, the Indian Green Building Council, set up by the Confederation of Indian Industry. And GRIHA, developed by TERI and adopted as India's national rating system.
Both are built around Indian codes and climate zones in a way an international system structurally can't be. GRIHA is even required for many central government buildings.

# scene photos=india_skyline:2 grade=day
The trade-off is recognition. A GRIHA rating carries real weight with an Indian regulator or developer, and much less with an international investor comparing a portfolio across three continents.

> chapter: 2 · What does "certified green" mean?
(pause 1.0)
So here's the uncomfortable question under all of this. If these systems measure different things, weight them differently, and were built for different economies, what does "certified green" actually mean when you see it on a wall?

# scene photos=new_green_building,old_building grade=day
> quote: Certified against a standard, not certified "equal". @ specific
It means the building was assessed against a specific, published standard, through a specific process, in a specific context. That's it.
It doesn't mean "equally green" across systems, any more than a five-star hotel in one country is automatically equal to a five-star hotel somewhere else.
The star system isn't fake. The comparison across systems is where people get misled.

# scene photos=solar_roof,concrete_pour grade=day
> versus: OPERATIONAL | EMBODIED
And then there's the carbon question itself. Most systems were designed around operational energy, the power a building uses every day. Embodied carbon, the emissions locked into concrete, steel and glass, has only recently started to get serious weight.
EDGE counts embodied energy in materials from the start. Newer versions of the other systems are moving the same way, and that shift is where the real carbon war is being fought.

# scene photos=site_inspection,checklist grade=day
> chapter: 3 · The part the plaque can't tell you
> label: Verification is where a rating earns its credibility @ verification
This is where verification earns its keep. A rating is only as credible as the checking behind it.
Someone confirming that the fixture that was specified is the fixture that was installed. That the sensor that was modelled is actually wired in. That the solar capacity on paper matches what's bolted to the roof.

# scene photos=tablet_site grade=day
> item: EDGE: third-party site audit before final certificate @ third-party
> item: Others: documentation review, site checks vary by system @ varies
How much of that happens on site varies by system. EDGE, for example, requires a neutral third-party auditor to visit the finished building before the final certificate. Other systems lean more on submitted documentation, with site checks that vary by system and credit.
Strip the verification layer out, and any of these systems becomes a paperwork exercise. Keep it in, and even a less famous system can mean more in practice than a prestigious plaque whose details nobody checked on site.

# scene photos=lobby grade=night zoom=out
> quote: Ask what it required, and who checked. @ required
So next time you see one of these plaques, the better question isn't "which certification is the best". It's "what did this specific system require, and did someone verify it survived the real construction site?"
That's the part the plaque can't tell you. That's the part you have to ask about.
