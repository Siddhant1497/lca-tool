> title: A Week Inside a Green Building Audit
People think auditing a building for green certification is like inspecting a hotel room. Walk in, look around, nod, leave with a checklist.
It is nothing like that.

# scene photos=blueprints,tablet_site grade=day
> chapter: Day 1 · The paper building
> label: Forensic, not ceremonial @ forensic
Here's what it actually is. It's forensic.
You arrive with a stack of drawings and specifications that represent a building's original intentions. Then you spend days finding every place reality quietly drifted from the plan.

# scene photos=subcontractors grade=day
> chapter: Day 3 · How the gap happens
(pause 0.8)
And it's almost never anyone's fault, not in the villain sense.
A sustainability feature gets specified in a design meeting, where it's easy to say yes. Then it has to survive a budget review six months later. A procurement delay. A contractor building to a standard they've genuinely never worked with before.
By the time I'm standing there with a checklist, that feature has run an obstacle course most people never see.

# scene photos=checklist grade=day
> quote: Assume intentions erode. Verify what survived. @ eroded
So the job isn't rewarding good intentions. It's the opposite. You assume good intentions get eroded by default, and you verify what actually made it through intact.

That means evidence. Photographs of installed equipment. Product data sheets and purchase invoices that match the specification. Test and commissioning reports showing systems were actually set up to run the way they were modelled.

And sometimes reality is better than the plan. A project where the client added extra solar capacity late, and the roof ended up generating more than the model assumed. Those are good days, and they deserve to be recorded just as carefully.

# scene photos=finished_building_people grade=day
> chapter: Day 5 · What never makes the press release
This is the part that never makes it into a press release. Nobody puts "the auditor confirmed the sensors were wired correctly" on a building's marketing material.
But that's the real difference between a certification that means something and one that's decoration. Not the ambition on the drawings. The proof that the ambition survived.
