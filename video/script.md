# What Happens When Every Building on Earth Has to Hit Net Zero?

*Video essay script — Aperture-style format*
*Estimated runtime: 12-14 minutes*

---

## ACT 1 — THE HOOK (0:00–1:00)

**[VISUAL: slow drone push-in on a dense city skyline at dawn, fog rolling between towers]**

Right now, wherever you're watching this, you're probably inside a building.

Take a second and look around. The walls. The ceiling. The concrete under the floor, the steel somewhere inside it, the glass in front of you. None of it feels like it's doing anything. It's just... there. Holding you up, keeping the weather out.

**[VISUAL: slow zoom on a single window, city lights reflected]**

But that building you're sitting in, and every other building on the planet, is quietly responsible for more of the climate crisis than every car, truck, ship, and plane combined.

**[BEAT — hold on black or a slow fade]**

So here's the question this video is actually about. Not "should buildings go green." That question was settled years ago. The real question is much harder: what does it actually take for every building on Earth, all of them, the ones standing today and the ones not yet built, to hit net zero? And is it even possible in the time we have left?

---

## ACT 2 — THE SCALE OF THE PROBLEM (1:00–3:30)

**[VISUAL: data visualization, a bar breaking down global emissions by sector]**

Start with the number that should be much more famous than it is. Buildings and construction account for roughly 37 percent of global CO2 emissions, and 28 percent of global energy use.

**[VISUAL: aerial timelapse of a city expanding over years, or a construction site with cranes]**

And the sector isn't shrinking, it's expanding. In 2024 alone, the world added floor space equivalent to building a new Paris every single week. Global floor area grew about 20 percent between 2015 and 2024. That's not a typo. That's just how much of the world is still being built, largely in Asia and Africa, where entire cities are going up in the same decade we're supposed to be cutting emissions in half.

**[VISUAL: split into two labeled halves — a lit building interior / a pile of raw concrete and rebar]**

Here's where it gets more complicated than "just use solar panels." Buildings actually carry two separate carbon problems, and most people, even people in the industry, only think about one of them.

The first is **operational carbon**. That's the energy a building burns every single day it exists: the air conditioning, the lighting, the elevators, the boiler. This is the part net metering and rooftop solar can chip away at.

The second is **embodied carbon**, and this one is brutal. It's the emissions baked into the materials before the building even opens its doors. Cement and steel alone account for roughly 18 percent of *all* global emissions, not just building emissions, all of them. And raw material extraction for construction now drives close to half of the entire world's material-related emissions.

**[BEAT]**

So a "net zero building" that runs entirely on renewable energy can still have detonated its entire multi-decade carbon budget on the day it was poured.

---

## ACT 3 — WHY THIS IS SO HARD TO FIX (3:30–7:00)

**[VISUAL: split screen, an old low-rise apartment block next to a glass tower]**

Here's the uncomfortable part. Most of the buildings that will exist in 2050, the deadline the world has more or less agreed on for net zero, are already standing today. You cannot demolish and rebuild your way out of this. It has to be fixed while people are living and working inside it.

**[VISUAL: retrofit footage, workers installing insulation, heat pumps]**

That means retrofitting, at a scale nobody has ever attempted. Re-insulating walls, replacing gas boilers with heat pumps, swapping out old glazing, on buildings that in some cases are centuries old. And that's before you even touch the newer problem: half of all new buildings going up today still have no energy code applied to them at all.

**[VISUAL: line graph, buildings sector emissions trajectory vs required pathway, showing a growing gap]**

The numbers show exactly how far off track this is. To stay aligned with net zero, operational emissions from buildings need to fall by more than half from where they are right now, by 2030. Instead, in the most recent full year of data, they hit a new record high. The renewable share of the energy buildings run on needs to reach 46 percent by the end of the decade. Right now it sits at about 17 percent.

**[VISUAL: slow pan across a map, national flags fading in and out]**

And almost no country has a real plan for this. As of the most recent reporting, not a single G20 country has submitted a serious, detailed national strategy for decarbonizing its building sector. Two countries have building codes that are actually aligned with a zero-emissions trajectory. Two.

**[BEAT — this is the emotional low point of the video]**

So when people say "buildings just need to go green," what they're actually asking for is this: retrofit a hundred years of existing infrastructure, rebuild the entire cement and steel supply chain, rewrite building codes in nearly two hundred countries, and do it all inside about twenty-five years, most of which is already gone.

---

## ACT 4 — WHAT ACTUALLY WORKS (7:00–10:30)

**[VISUAL: shift in tone and lighting, warmer, daylight instead of dusk]**

Here's the part that doesn't get talked about enough. This isn't science fiction. Every piece of this already exists, and some of it is already working.

**[VISUAL: a certified green building, LEED/EDGE plaque, solar array on roof]**

Certification systems like LEED, EDGE, IGBC, and GRIHA exist specifically to force a building through this accounting, both operational and embodied, before it ever opens. They're not perfect and they're not universal, but wherever they've been adopted at scale, they've dragged the entire local construction industry's baseline upward, because once one developer in a market builds to that standard, competitors have to follow to stay competitive.

**[VISUAL: mass timber construction, cross-laminated timber panels being installed]**

On the materials side, the shift is already underway: mass timber replacing steel and concrete in structures where it's viable, low-carbon and geopolymer concrete cutting cement's footprint by 30 to 70 percent, recycled steel loops that are dramatically less carbon-intensive than virgin steel.

**[VISUAL: a heat pump installation, a smart thermostat, a building energy dashboard]**

On the operational side, electrification plus a cleaner grid does most of the heavy lifting. A heat pump running on a grid that's even half-renewable beats a gas boiler by a wide margin, and that math only improves as the grid keeps decarbonizing underneath it, without the building itself changing at all.

**[BEAT]**

The honest version of the story isn't "we don't know how." It's "we know exactly how, and we're moving at roughly a third of the speed we need to."

---

## ACT 5 — THE CLOSE (10:30–13:00)

**[VISUAL: return to the opening shot, but now daylight, the fog has cleared]**

So go back to that building you're sitting in right now. Somewhere, someone decided what it would be made of, how it would be heated, how much energy it would waste before anyone ever noticed. Those were just decisions. Not laws of physics. Decisions, made under normal, forgettable, everyday pressure to finish on time and under budget.

**[VISUAL: slow pull back, the city from far above, lights beginning to switch off building by building as dawn breaks]**

Every building on Earth will eventually be rebuilt, retrofitted, or replaced. That's not a prediction, that's just what happens to buildings over a long enough timeline. The only real question left is whether that turnover happens fast enough, and whether the next version of the building you're in right now is built by someone who already knew better.

**[HOLD ON BLACK]**

## PRODUCTION NOTES

- **Tone:** measured, slightly grave, narrator never raises their voice. Long pauses do the emotional work, not music swells.
- **Pacing:** slow cuts (3-5 second holds on wide shots), faster cuts only during the "scale of the problem" data section.
- **Music:** minimal ambient/drone under narration, swelling only in Act 5.
- **Data citations to keep on screen briefly when stated:** 37% global emissions (UNEP GlobalABC, 2025-26 report), 18% cement+steel share, 46% renewable target vs ~17% current, operational emissions needing to fall 56% by 2030. Verify these against the latest GlobalABC report before publishing, since this is a fast-moving figure.
- **Where your own expertise adds authority the reference channel can't get elsewhere:** the embodied vs. operational carbon distinction, how certifications like LEED/EDGE actually verify this rather than just claim it, and what a retrofit actually involves on the ground. That's the section worth spending your own case-study detail on if you want to differentiate from a purely narrated explainer.
