Carbon · 2 November 2026

CBAM for Indian exporters: what changed in January 2026

5 min read

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism moved from reporting to paying on 1 January 2026. It covers iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity imported into the EU. The legal duty sits with the EU importer, but the data has to come from the producer. For Indian exporters, that makes emissions data part of the product.

What has changed

  • EU importers now need authorised CBAM declarant status before they import covered goods.
  • Importers bringing in less than 50 tonnes of covered goods a year are exempt, after the EU's 2025 simplification. Hydrogen and electricity are excluded from that threshold.
  • CBAM certificates for 2026 imports are bought from 2027, with the first annual declaration due in 2027.
  • Where verified actual emissions are missing, default values apply. They are deliberately conservative, so they usually cost the buyer more.

What exporters should do now

  • Map which of your products fall under CBAM, by customs code.
  • Calculate embedded emissions per product and installation, including precursors such as clinker or pig iron.
  • Have the figures verified, and keep the records your buyer will need for their declaration.
  • Use the same data for India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme if your plant is covered.

Buyers are already comparing suppliers on this. An exporter with verified, lower emissions saves their customer money.

Reporting · 2 November 2026

BRSR Core is coming for your suppliers too

4 min read

SEBI's BRSR Core is a short list of key ESG indicators, from emissions to water and waste, that listed companies must have independently assured or assessed. It has been phased in by market capitalisation: the top 150 companies from FY 2023-24, then 250, then 500, and India's top 1,000 listed companies by FY 2026-27.

Why suppliers should care

SEBI recalibrated the value-chain requirements in March 2025, and those disclosures are phasing in more slowly. In practice, large listed companies are already asking their main suppliers for energy, emissions and water data, because their own numbers depend on it.

What a supplier can do this year

  • Build a Scope 1 and 2 inventory with evidence behind every figure: meter readings, fuel invoices and the official grid emission factor.
  • Track water withdrawal and waste by type, with the same discipline.
  • Prepare one data pack you can reuse for every customer questionnaire.
  • Start on Scope 3 categories your customers will ask about first, such as purchased goods and transport.

The companies that have this ready become the easy supplier to keep.

Design · 2 November 2026

Why west façades need more than horizontal fins

3 min read

Horizontal fins work well when the sun is high. On a south façade in Ahmedabad at 1 pm in May, the sun is around 76° up, and 300 mm deep fins at 300 mm spacing shade the glass almost completely.

Turn the same façade to face west and look at 5 pm on the same day. The sun is only about 21° above the horizon and almost straight in front of the glass. The profile angle drops to around 22°, and the same fins now shade only about 40% of it. That's exactly when the room is hottest and the cooling load peaks.

What works better on east and west

  • Vertical fins, angled to block the low sun while keeping the view.
  • A jaali or perforated screen a short distance in front of the glass.
  • Less glass on these façades, and glass with a lower solar heat gain coefficient where you can't avoid it.
  • Planning the plan: putting stairs, cores and services on the west side.
